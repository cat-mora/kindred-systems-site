How quickly should a business respond to a new enquiry?

Enquiry response and missed calls 4 min read · Published 20 September 2026 · Updated 20 September 2026

Reply as quickly as you can without rushing or giving the wrong information. During business hours, think in minutes or hours rather than days, especially when the enquiry is urgent or the person is contacting several providers. After hours, confirm that the message arrived and say when a person will reply. Then make sure somebody does.

The moment somebody sends an enquiry is often when they are most ready to talk. They may be contacting several providers, dealing with an urgent problem or finally acting on something they have put off for months. A fast response tells them the business is organised. A quick but irrelevant automated message does not. Decide what the first reply should include and which staff member is responsible for sending it.

What slows down the first reply

Response delays are commonly caused by fragmented channels, not a lack of goodwill. Calls go to voicemail, form notifications arrive in a shared inbox, social messages sit with marketing, and staff assume someone else replied. Automatic messages create the appearance of speed but may not answer the person's question. Map every entry point and test it from the prospect's side, including evenings, weekends and staff leave.

  • Nobody can see all new enquiries in one queue or report their age.

  • The business measures the automated receipt time as if it were a human response.

  • Missed callers receive no text or return-call expectation.

  • Replies ask people to repeat information they already provided.

  • Urgent and routine enquiries are handled in the same order.

Set a response standard the team can meet

Create a service standard the team can meet consistently. Triage urgent, sensitive and high-intent enquiries; acknowledge all others; and route each one to a named person. Give staff approved responses for common situations, but leave space to reflect what the person said. If the business promises a call by a certain time, that commitment should be visible and reassigned when the owner becomes unavailable.

1. List every phone number, form, email address, chat tool, marketplace and social channel that can receive an enquiry.

2. Set staffed-hours and after-hours expectations by channel and urgency, with a named backup owner.

3. Capture the enquiry in one working view, including source, time, need, consent and next action.

4. Use an immediate acknowledgement only to confirm receipt and set expectations, not to pretend a conversation has occurred.

5. Review overdue enquiries and sample reply quality, then adjust capacity or routing where delays repeat.

What to monitor

Measure the time to a human reply separately from the automatic acknowledgement. Also check how many people are reached, how many book and whether promised callbacks happened. An average can hide a few enquiries that waited far too long, so review overdue messages as well. Compare phone, website and social enquiries separately. Speed should never come at the cost of careless advice or professional duties.

What gets in the way

  • Promising 24-hour availability when nobody is responsible for monitoring it.

  • Sending an instant link with no context when the person asked a specific question.

  • Calling repeatedly without permission or leaving messages that disclose sensitive information.

  • Optimising for speed while staff lack the knowledge or authority to help.

  • Assuming every enquiry is non-urgent; legal, health and financial contexts may require triage and escalation rules.

Common questions

Is an automated reply enough?

It is useful as a receipt and expectation setter. It is not a substitute for a human response when the person needs judgement, reassurance or an answer specific to their situation.

What if we cannot respond within minutes?

State a realistic timeframe and give the person a safe next option where relevant. Consistency and honesty are better than a promise the team regularly misses.

Should we call or email first?

Use the channel the person requested when possible, and consider the sensitivity of the enquiry. If no preference is known, a short message can confirm who will call and when, reducing surprise and missed connections.

A realistic example

Example: A family law firm receives enquiries through phone calls, web forms and a directory. Calls are handled quickly, but directory emails are checked only once a day. The firm brings all enquiries into one list, gives urgent safety concerns to trained staff and tells each person when somebody will respond. This example is general information, not legal advice.
