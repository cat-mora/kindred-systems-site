Why do good leads stop responding or disappear after an enquiry?

Follow-up and long buying cycles 4 min read · Published 20 September 2026 · Updated 20 September 2026

Good leads stop responding when life gets in the way or the business leaves important questions unanswered. They may be worried about price, risk, timing, being judged or choosing the wrong provider. Some are still interested but do not know what to do next. Review the last conversation before deciding they were a poor lead or only shopping around.

No reply can mean many things. The person may have decided against it, become busy, felt overwhelmed, needed to ask somebody else or chosen the provider who made the decision easier. With an expensive or personal service, hesitation is normal. A health concern, legal dispute, property move or building project does not stop the rest of life from happening around it. Make it easy for people to return to the conversation when they are ready.

Why people stop replying

Go back to the last real conversation. Did the person receive a price with no explanation? Did they leave a consultation with unanswered questions? Were they promised a call that never came? Did the follow-up have any connection to what they had said? If the team did not ask about timing, concerns or other people involved in the decision, it cannot know why the person stopped replying.

  • A large number of people stop replying as soon as they receive a price, proposal or treatment plan.

  • People are asked to book without knowing what the appointment will involve.

  • The notes list calls and emails but say little about what the person is worried about.

  • Every follow-up repeats the offer instead of answering a question.

  • People who return later say they thought the business had forgotten them.

Make it easier to answer

Talk about the decision before sending the paperwork. Explain what the proposal or plan will contain and arrange a time to discuss it. Ask what the person will need to feel comfortable deciding and whether somebody else needs to be involved. If they stop replying, send a short message that gives them an easy choice: talk now, come back later or tell you they are no longer interested.

1. Find the point where people most often stop replying and review what happened beforehand.

2. Look for repeated concerns about fit, timing, money, trust or understanding.

3. Improve the conversation before people stop replying, rather than adding more reminders afterwards.

4. Send a final message that makes it easy to give an honest answer.

5. Use the answers to improve your advertising, consultations and proposals.

What you can learn from silence

Track how many people start replying again, what they do next and the reasons they give. Keep their own words in the notes instead of forcing every answer into a broad lost category. When people say they need time, ask what they are waiting for. Finance approval, another quote, a family conversation or a project date will each call for different follow-up.

What not to do

  • Assuming silence means the price was too high without asking.

  • Sending more messages when the messages themselves are not helpful.

  • Using fear or urgency around a sensitive personal decision.

  • Sending a proposal and leaving the person to interpret it alone.

  • Treating not now as a permanent no.

Common questions

Should we ask why they chose someone else?

Yes, if the question is brief, respectful and optional. Ask because you want to learn, not because you want to argue with their decision.

Does ghosting mean our leads are poor quality?

Sometimes an enquiry is not a good fit. But if people repeatedly stop replying at the same point, look at what your business is doing before blaming lead quality.

What should the final message say?

Identify yourself, refer to the enquiry, acknowledge that plans change and give them a few simple options. Tell them how to contact you later and include any required opt-out.

A realistic example

Example: A commercial law prospect receives a detailed engagement proposal after a positive call but no scheduled review. The partner assumes price is the issue. A follow-up asks for a brief discussion of scope and timing, and the team begins asking those questions before future proposals. This is an illustrative example and not legal advice or a claimed outcome.
