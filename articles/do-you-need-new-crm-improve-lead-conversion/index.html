Do you need a new CRM to improve lead conversion?

CRM, automation and AI 4 min read · Published 20 September 2026 · Updated 20 September 2026

Not necessarily. Many businesses already have software that can track enquiries, but the team is not using it consistently or nobody is responsible for follow-up. Other businesses have a customer database that starts only after somebody buys. Review what you need first. Keep the current system if it can do the job, and change or add software only when it cannot.

CRM projects often begin with frustration: enquiries are missed, reports are unreliable and the team keeps separate lists. Software can solve some of that, but it cannot decide who owns a new lead, what a useful follow-up looks like or when a person needs a human response. Changing platforms without those decisions can recreate the same problem with more expense and disruption.

The software may not be the problem

Work out whether the software is missing an important function or the team is not using what it already has. The system may be unable to capture certain enquiries, create follow-up tasks, protect sensitive information or produce the reports you need. In other cases, those features exist but nobody has set them up or trained the team. Some databases also work well for customers but have no reliable place for people who are still deciding.

  • New leads live in email, calendars, phones or spreadsheets until they buy.

  • The CRM contains contacts but no visible next action or lead owner.

  • Automation sends messages that staff do not know about.

  • Reports depend on optional fields that are rarely completed.

  • The team asks for a new platform, but each person describes a different process.

Check the system you already have

Write down what the business needs before comparing brands. Test whether the current system can capture enquiries, assign follow-up, send reminders and produce the reports you need. Also consider security, integrations, data quality, cost and how much work the team will need to use it. If the system cannot do the job, compare other platforms against that list using real examples from your business.

1. List where enquiries come from, who follows them up and what needs to be recorded.

2. Check the licences, integrations and features you already pay for.

3. Separate what the business needs now from features that would simply be nice to have.

4. Test the most important follow-up process in the current system if you can.

5. Compare the full cost and effort of keeping, adding to or replacing the system.

How to judge the options

Judge the system by whether staff use it and whether fewer enquiries are forgotten. Check that every enquiry is captured, assigned quickly and given a follow-up date. Also watch for overdue tasks, duplicate records, consent problems and automated messages sent in error. A simple system that the team uses can work better than sophisticated software they avoid.

Before you buy another CRM

  • Choosing software before writing down what the business needs it to do.

  • Using a customer database that has no reliable place for people who have not bought yet.

  • Moving poor-quality data without deciding what should be kept and whether contact is permitted.

  • Automating unclear or non-compliant messages.

  • Assuming the software company's standard setup will suit a specialised service.

Common questions

Can Kindred Systems work with our current CRM?

Yes, when it can support the agreed process. Recommendations can use the current system, another platform or an additional managed system, depending on the need.

What if we have no lead database?

Create a suitable pre-client process that captures enquiries, consent, stage, owner and next action. It may sit within an existing platform or a separate connected system.

How do we avoid buying features we do not need?

Use a requirement scorecard tied to client experience, team work and reporting. Test real scenarios before committing.

A realistic example

Example: A cosmetic clinic uses practice software for booked patients, but web enquiries remain in a shared inbox until an appointment is made. Instead of replacing the clinical system, it considers adding a separate way to assign enquiries, record consent and stop follow-up once somebody books. This example is not medical or software purchasing advice.
