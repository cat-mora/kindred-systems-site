How quickly should a law firm respond to a new client enquiry?

Lead conversion in specific industries 4 min read · Published 20 September 2026 · Updated 20 September 2026

A law firm should acknowledge a new enquiry promptly and triage any urgent deadline, safety or conflict issue through an approved intake process. During staffed hours, speed should be measured in minutes or hours, not days, but the first contact must not create an unintended retainer or request unnecessary confidential detail. State who will respond next, when and what information is needed.

People often contact a lawyer when time, money, family or reputation feels exposed. A slow or confusing response can add distress and send a suitable client elsewhere. Yet legal intake cannot be treated like an ordinary retail callback. The firm needs enough information to route the enquiry and assess urgency while protecting confidentiality, managing conflicts and avoiding premature advice.

What the first response needs to do

Map phone, website, referral, directory and after-hours enquiries. Identify which channels ask for sensitive details and who monitors them. Create escalation rules for court dates, limitation concerns, safety, urgent transactions and other time-sensitive issues relevant to the practice. The rules should be prepared with the firm's professional obligations and jurisdiction in mind.

  • Voicemail does not say when messages are checked.

  • Web forms ask for the person's full story before the firm has checked for a conflict.

  • Reception staff do not know which information suggests the enquiry may be urgent.

  • Partners receive enquiries directly, with nobody covering them during court or leave.

  • People have to repeat the same information to several staff members.

Build a safe intake process

Keep the first contact simple. Confirm that the enquiry arrived, collect only the information needed to send it to the right person and explain that the firm has not yet agreed to act or give advice. A trained staff member can then check conflicts, urgency and whether the firm can help before arranging a consultation. Record who is responsible and when the person was promised a reply.

1. List every phone number, form, email address and directory that receives enquiries, then assign a main and backup staff member.

2. Teach staff which information requires urgent attention and who they should tell.

3. Collect only the information needed at that point, with suitable privacy and conflict checks.

4. Tell the person who will contact them, when and what will happen next.

5. Review response times, missed enquiries and cases the firm could not accept.

What to track

Track how long people wait for confirmation and a trained staff member, how many book consultations and how many suitable cases the firm accepts. Review overdue callbacks, complaints and questions staff were unsure how to handle. Separate cases the firm could not take from enquiries lost through delay. Professional duties and rules about forming a retainer vary, so the firm's legal and risk advisors should approve the wording and process.

What to avoid

  • Offering legal conclusions before conflicts and engagement are addressed.

  • Leaving urgent enquiries in a general inbox.

  • Collecting extensive confidential information through insecure channels.

  • Treating every case as urgent or promising a lawyer call the firm cannot deliver.

  • Failing to give unsuitable enquirers a clear closure or appropriate referral option where permitted.

Common questions

Is an instant automated reply enough?

No. It can confirm receipt and set expectations, but urgent and sensitive enquiries require human triage under the firm's approved process.

Should intake staff ask about deadlines?

The firm should define the minimum questions and escalation steps with professional input. Staff should not interpret legal rights beyond their role.

Can a chatbot qualify legal leads?

It may collect limited routing information if privacy, disclosure and supervision are sound, but it should not give unsupervised legal advice or imply a solicitor-client relationship.

A realistic example

Example: A commercial law firm receives a web enquiry late in the day about an upcoming transaction. An automatic reply says that the firm has not agreed to act and explains when messages are checked. A trained staff member checks urgency and conflicts, then confirms when a lawyer will call. This example is general information, not legal advice.
