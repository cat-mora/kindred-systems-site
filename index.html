What this looks like inside the business.

  • AI adoption is inconsistent across teams.
  • Staff are experimenting, and it still feels like pilot purgatory.
  • Nobody can tell you what the AI spend earned.
  • Marketing’s tried five different AI tools this year, and nobody can say if any of them sold anything.
  • The AI chatbot on the website hasn’t moved a single sales number.
  • The platform everyone was excited about is gathering dust.
  • Leadership doesn’t know where AI would make the biggest difference.
Why this keeps happening

It’s rarely a technology problem.

  • Tools get bought before the business problem is defined.
  • Adoption stalls because there’s no plan for how people use it day to day.
  • Without a baseline, ROI can’t be measured, only guessed at.
  • Too many experiments run at once, with no way to prioritise between them.
  • Nobody’s agreed on who owns AI or what the rules are, so it comes down to luck.
What changes

Picture six months from now.

Revenue is up, or costs are down, or both, and you can say exactly why. The board stops asking what the AI spend bought, because you already told them. The platform everyone was excited about a year ago is doing real work instead of gathering dust. A decision that used to sit in someone’s inbox for three weeks now gets made in three days, because somebody owns it and there’s a number attached to it. Staff who spent hours a week on repetitive admin, copying information between systems, chasing approvals, updating spreadsheets by hand, are doing less of that and more of the work that needs their judgement.

  • More money in: sales that used to stall now close
  • Less money out: the tool you’re already paying for finally earns its keep
  • Hours back: staff freed from repetitive admin
  • One clear owner, so decisions stop waiting for someone to sign off
  • A straight answer, ready before the board asks for one

In a nutshell: save money, make more money, or both.

How Kindred approaches AI

How this gets done, in practice.

Every engagement starts with what you already believe you need AI to do, and that’s usually a good starting brief. From there, the work is finding whatever inside that brief will do the most for your cost, your revenue, or the value of the business itself, using the tools you already have rather than a new piece of software to buy. Sometimes that’s exactly what you expected. Sometimes it becomes clear once the work is underway that the bigger opportunity sits somewhere slightly different, and that gets worked through together, not sprung on you afterwards. Either way, what’s left behind is practical: a process, a short set of rules, and training your own people can run, so your team keeps it going instead of paying an adviser to stay in the room.
